RUBRIC GUIDE
================================================================================

Learn how to write objective, measurable, and timeless evaluation criteria that properly test AI shopping assistant responses.

WHAT IS A RUBRIC?
-----------------
A rubric is a scoring guide — a list of specific things to look for when evaluating an AI's response. Rubrics turn subjective questions ("Was this response good?") into objective ones ("Did this response do X, Y, and Z?").

Example: Instead of asking "Did the AI give good laptop recommendations?", we check specific criteria like "Recommends at least one laptop with 16GB RAM or more" ✓ or ✗

RUBRIC FORMAT
-------------
Every rubric criterion follows this format:
- Item X:
- Weight: +5 / +3 / +1 / −1 / −3 / −5
- Criterion: Self-contained verb-led requirement

First Criterion Is Always Link Validity: Your rubric must always begin with: Weight: +5 — "Provides website links that are valid and lead to product-specific pages."

UNDERSTANDING THE WEIGHT SYSTEM
-------------------------------
Each criterion gets a weight that reflects its importance:

+5: Critical requirement. The response fails without this.
    Example: Recommends the correct product category

+3: Important requirement. Significantly improves response quality.
    Example: Matches a specific material requirement

+1: Nice to have. Helpful but not essential.
    Example: Provides a maintenance or care detail

-1: Minor deviation. Slightly reduces quality.
    Example: Mentions a non-preferred but not harmful colorway

-3: Problematic behavior. Detracts from quality.
    Example: Recommends an item that doesn't match size requirements

-5: Serious failure. Breaks the task.
    Example: Recommends a completely incompatible product

POSITIVE VS. NEGATIVE CRITERIA
------------------------------

Positive Criteria (+1, +3, +5):
Reward the AI for meeting explicit or implied requirements.
Examples:
- Recommends at least one laptop with 16GB RAM or more
- Recommends at least one coat with a water-resistant exterior such as polyester or nylon
- Lists dimensions for each recommended table
Minimum required: 15 positive criteria

Negative Criteria (−1, −3, −5):
Penalize harmful or incorrect behavior. Negative criteria must test something the model did, not something it failed to do.
Examples:
- Recommends any backpack with trekking-pole attachment loops
- Mentions any dining table shorter than 25 inches in height
Not allowed: "Fails to include a laptop with 16GB RAM"
Instead, use a positive criterion: "Recommends at least one laptop with 16GB RAM"
Minimum required: 3 negative criteria

Rubric Size Requirements: Your rubric must contain between 18 and 40 total items. This includes at least 15 positive and at least 3 negative criteria.

RULES FOR GOOD CRITERIA
-----------------------

1. Verb-Led Criteria
Every criterion must start with an action verb: Recommends, Identifies, Specifies, Lists, Describes, Includes, Explains, Provides, Compares, Notes
BAD: "Laptop has good battery life"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one laptop with a battery capacity of 4500 mAh or greater"

2. Self-Contained Criteria
Each criterion must stand alone. A reader must be able to evaluate it without seeing the prompt, the model answer, images, or other criteria. Never reference "the requirement above" or "the photo."
BAD: "Matches the requirement stated in the prompt"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one phone case in a pink colorway"

3. Specific & Measurable Criteria
Use explicit, objective details. Describe measurable attributes (like dimensions), not subjective outcomes.
If you're using measurements, they just need to be plausible. So look it up if you aren't sure. E.g. look up "medium sized bag dimensions" and use those.
BAD: "Recommends a roomy bag"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one bag with a compartment measuring at least 12 × 6 × 6 inches"

4. Timelessness
Criteria must work years from now. Never include price, sales, stock status, release years, ratings, reviews, popularity, shipping times, or seasonal exclusives. Use timeless attributes like materials, dimensions, specs, and compatibility.
BAD: "Recommends a laptop under $1000"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one laptop that weighs 3.5 pounds or less"

5. No Double Negatives
A criterion must not contain a double negative, which makes evaluation ambiguous. Use clear, single-direction requirements.
BAD: "Does not recommend any chair that is not stable"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair with a listed weight capacity of at least 250 pounds"

6. Criteria Must Be Atomic
Each item must test one measurable thing. If you're using "and" or "rather than" to combine multiple requirements, split them into separate criteria.
BAD: "Recommends at least one chair upholstered in fabric or mesh rather than leather"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair upholstered in fabric or mesh"
GOOD: "Recommends any chair upholstered in leather (negative criterion)"
GOOD: Alternatively, split into three: "...upholstered in fabric" + "...upholstered in mesh" + negative for leather

7. No Justification in Criteria
Criteria must state only the requirement — never include reasoning or justification. Remove phrases like "to maintain...", "since the user...", "because...", or "for better...".
BAD: "Recommends at least one chair upholstered in fabric or mesh to maintain a casual living-room aesthetic"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair upholstered in fabric or mesh"

8. Translating Context into Criteria
You can translate prompt details into objective criteria, but never reference the profile you built in the prompt directly. Convert implicit context into measurable product attributes.
BAD: "Matches the user's love of cars"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one watch with a motorsport chronograph dial"

9. Evaluate Products, Not Writing Style
Criteria must evaluate the actual products recommended, not how the AI phrases things. The evaluator inspects the products, not the prose.
BAD: "Describes each product in precise, technical language"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one refrigerator with a capacity of 18 cubic feet or greater"

STACKED CRITERIA
----------------
A stacked criterion tries to test more than one measurable requirement at the same time. This makes evaluation ambiguous because a single ✓ / ✗ cannot fairly score multiple conditions.

How to Spot Stacked Criteria:
A stacked criterion almost always contains words like "and," "as well as," "rather than," "both," or multiple numeric thresholds in one sentence.
- Uses "and" to join two measurable requirements
- Uses "or" in a way that evaluates more than one condition at once
- Compares two different attributes in one line
- Tests two product features but gives only one score

Why Stacked Criteria Are Bad:
- Ambiguous scoring: If one part is satisfied and another is not, evaluators cannot assign a clear score.
- Hidden double-counting: It becomes unclear which specific rule the model violated.
- Breaks rubric independence: Each criterion must evaluate one atomic requirement. Stacking creates dependencies.
- Harder to debug: If a stacked criterion fails, you can't tell which part the model missed.

Examples: Stacked vs. Atomic

BAD: "Recommends at least one chair with a seat width between 20 and 24 inches and a seat height between 16 and 18 inches"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair with a seat width between 20 and 24 inches"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair with a seat height between 16 and 18 inches"

BAD: "Recommends at least one stroller that reclines fully and weighs less than 20 pounds and includes an adjustable canopy"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one stroller that reclines fully"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one stroller that weighs less than 20 pounds"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one stroller that includes an adjustable canopy"

Quick Rule of Thumb: If a criterion contains "and," it is almost always stacked and needs to be split. Each rubric item must test one atomic, measurable property — never a bundle of them.

THE "NO INVERSES" RULE
----------------------
This rule is critical and often misunderstood. Rubrics must never include a positive criterion and a negative criterion that are simply opposites of each other — this results in testing the same requirement twice, which is invalid.

Not Allowed: Inverse Pairs
These pairs are invalid because they test the same condition from opposite directions. If a recommendation violates one, it automatically violates the other, which breaks rubric independence.

Invalid Pair #1:
Positive: Recommends at least one laptop with 16GB of RAM or more
Negative: Recommends any laptop with less than 16GB of RAM

Invalid Pair #2:
Positive: Recommends at least one backpack with a capacity of at least 25 liters
Negative: Recommends any backpack with a capacity below 25 liters

Invalid Pair #3:
Positive: Recommends at least one laptop with 16GB of RAM or more
Negative: Recommends any laptop with more than 32GB of RAM
The "or more" in the positive criterion includes all RAM values above 16GB, which means any laptop that violates the negative (by having more than 32GB) would still satisfy the positive. This creates an impossible standard where a recommendation could be penalized for having too much RAM while simultaneously being rewarded for the same attribute.

CRITERIA EVALUATE PRODUCT ATTRIBUTES — NOT THE MODEL'S WRITING
-------------------------------------------------------------
Rubrics must judge the products recommended, not how the AI describes them. The AI is allowed to use subjective phrasing such as "a warm red color" or "a sleek design." You must not write criteria that grade the AI's language style.

Not Allowed: Judging Wording, Not Product Attributes
BAD: "Describes each product using objective specifications"
BAD: "Explains why each item fits using measurable details only"

Allowed: Judging the Product Itself
GOOD: "Recommends at least one chair with a seat width of 20 inches or greater"
GOOD: "Recommends at least one phone case in a pink colorway"

The Rule: Criteria must be objective and explicit, but the model's language does not need to be. Even if the AI says "the color feels cheerful," evaluators look only at the actual colorway of the product — not the tone of the explanation. Only the recommended product attributes must satisfy the rubric.

EXPLICIT VS. IMPLIED REQUIREMENTS
---------------------------------
The best rubrics test both explicit requirements (things the user directly stated) and implied requirements (things a smart assistant should figure out from context).

Context in Prompt → Implied Requirement:
- "I'm a nurse who works 12-hour shifts" → Shoes with arch support or cushioned insoles
- "Shopping for my 80-year-old grandmother" → Products with large buttons, simple interfaces, or accessibility features
- "I live in a 400 sq ft studio apartment" → Furniture with compact dimensions or multi-functional design
- "I have three large dogs and need a new couch" → Couch made of polyester for stain resistance

FINAL RUBRIC CHECKLIST
----------------------
✓ Item 1 = Valid product links (+5)
✓ ≥15 positive + ≥3 negative criteria
✓ Total items between 18–40
✓ Verb-led, measurable, timeless, atomic, no justification
✓ No double negatives or positive/negative inverses
✓ Fully self-contained
✓ Evaluates product attributes, not AI writing style

PROMPT WRITING GUIDE
================================================================================

Learn how to write natural, human-sounding shopping prompts that create challenging and valuable evaluation rubrics.

WHAT MAKES A GOOD SHOPPING PROMPT?
----------------------------------
A good prompt should sound like something a real person would type into ChatGPT or Google when trying to buy something. It should be written in natural, informal language — not template-like, not over-polished, not robotic.

Think of how you would talk to a store associate if you had recently done a little research, but not enough to know exactly what to buy.

1. SOUND NATURAL AND HUMAN
--------------------------
Prompts should:
- Read like everyday messages
- Have relaxed grammar
- Include small imperfections or conversational phrasing
- Focus on the need, not on formal writing

Natural Phrasing:
- "I've been thinking about getting a new laptop for work but I'm not really sure what specs I need."
- "My old hair dryer died and I want something better this time — any ideas?"

Not Allowed:
- Prompts that look generated by ChatGPT templates
- Overly perfect academic writing
- Mechanical listing of attributes

2. USE BOTH EXPLICIT AND IMPLICIT REQUIREMENTS
----------------------------------------------
Good prompts include a mix of:
- Explicit details → directly stated requirements
- Implicit clues → personal context that indirectly shapes product needs

Explicit requirement example:
"I need blackout curtains that are at least 95 inches long."

Implicit requirement example:
"My bedroom gets a lot of sun in the morning and it wakes me up."
→ A smart assistant should infer that blackout or light-blocking curtains are needed.

Implicit Requirements = Difficulty: Implicit requirements are the #1 source of difficulty in the rubric. Most real humans DO NOT explicitly list every attribute. You should avoid making everything explicit.

3. USE REALISTIC SHOPPING SCENARIOS ONLY
----------------------------------------
Allowed:
- Buying a laptop
- Finding a used car
- Choosing a gift
- Selecting furniture
- Purchasing a stroller
- Buying specialty tools

Not Allowed:
- "I want to buy four houses today"
- "I'm shopping for 10 luxury cars at once"
- "I need the exact TV Taylor Swift has"

Prompts must reflect realistic human behavior.

4. INCLUDE AT LEAST 3 REQUIRED ATTRIBUTES
-----------------------------------------
Every category has required attributes (see Categories page). Your prompt must naturally mention at least 3 of them.

Example (Furniture):
- Size ("around 70 inches wide")
- Material ("fabric, not leather")
- Style ("mid-century")

Mention them naturally — no lists, no rigid formatting.

5. KEEP THE PROMPT COMPLETELY TIMELESS
--------------------------------------
Never Reference:
- Prices
- Sales, coupons, deals
- Availability, stock status
- Shipping speed
- Release dates
- "The newest version," "2024 model," etc.
- Anything tied to time ("this weekend," "next year")

Use Timeless Attributes:
- Materials
- Dimensions
- Capacity
- Ergonomics
- Colors
- Compatibility
- Weight limits
- Safety certifications

6. PROMPTS SHOULD NOT BE TOO SIMPLE
-----------------------------------
Overly generic:
"I need a new microwave. What should I buy?"

This Will NOT Produce Enough Rubric Items: Your prompt MUST contain enough detail (explicit or implicit) to create at least 18 strong rubric criteria, but preferably more.

7. DO NOT INTRODUCE DIFFICULTY WITH STRANGE LANGUAGE
----------------------------------------------------
Difficulty should come from:
- Implicit preferences
- Subtle functional needs
- Persona context
- Multiple constraints that interact

Difficulty Should NOT Come From:
- Contradictions ("I want something black but also bright white inside")
- Nonsense requirements
- Fake model numbers
- Awkward unnatural sentences
- Being intentionally confusing

8. PERSONAL DETAILS ARE OPTIONAL BUT USEFUL
-------------------------------------------
You can add light background details if they help shape the product needs. You can make up these details completely.

"I'm a grad student so I carry my laptop around every day."
→ Implies weight + durability matter.

But avoid long fictional biographies. Keep it short and relevant.

9. VARY THE PROMPT'S SPECIFICITY
--------------------------------
Not all prompts need the same level of detail. Different levels of specificity create different types of challenge:

Vague (still valid):
"I want a new blender — something strong enough for smoothies but not super loud."

Directional:
"I make smoothies every morning with frozen fruit and ice. My current blender struggles, so I need something with more power but not bulky."

Explicit:
"I need a 64-oz blender with at least 1200 watts and a tamper."

All three types can be good prompts as long as they meet the rules.

10. YOUR PROMPT MUST ALWAYS ENABLE A DIFFICULT RUBRIC
-----------------------------------------------------
The prompt must contain enough attributes, needs, context, and implicit requirements to justify:
- 15+ positive criteria
- 3+ negative criteria
- Many opportunities for model failure

If the Prompt Is Too Thin: You cannot complete the task if your prompt doesn't have enough detail to support 18–40 rubric items.

SUMMARY: WHAT MAKES A GOOD PROMPT
---------------------------------

A Good Prompt Is:
✓ Natural
✓ Human-sounding
✓ Includes explicit + implicit details
✓ Realistic
✓ Timeless
✓ Contains ≥ 3 attributes
✓ Rich enough for 18–40 rubric items
✓ Interesting/reasonable—not weird

A Good Prompt Is NOT:
✗ Robotic
✗ Overly polished
✗ Overly generic
✗ Overly specific
✗ Time-dependent
✗ Fictional or unrealistic
✗ Repetitive
✗ Contradictory

REVIEWER INSTRUCTIONS
================================================================================

Reviewers are the final checkpoint for quality. Your contributions directly shape the dataset that trains AI shopping assistants.

WHAT REVIEWERS DO
-----------------
- Ensure Quality Standards: You are the final safeguard before the task becomes training data.
- Correct the Task: There is no revision step. If fixable in under 30 minutes, you fix it yourself.
- Add Constructive Feedback: Fellows don't see your inline edits—they only see your written notes.
- Rate Before Editing: Provide a 1–5 quality rating before making any edits.

REVIEW WORKFLOW
---------------
Attempter (Creates task) → Review Round 1 (Fixes issues, rates Attempt) → Review Round 2 (Reviews R1, rates R1) → Approved (Becomes training data)

Role Flexibility: Reviewers are not "above" Attempters. You may be doing only reviewing, only attempting, or a mix of both. Roles shift based on accuracy, attention to detail, and quality of feedback.

HOW TO ACCESS & CLAIM TASKS
---------------------------
1. Go to the Annotation Dashboard
2. In the top-left you'll see:
   - My Tasks — tasks you created or are reviewing
   - Available Tasks — tasks you can claim
3. Claim a task from Available Tasks.

You will never be asked to review your own task. If it ever appears, simply skip it.

HOW TO PERFORM A REVIEW
-----------------------
1. Read the Entire Task
   Read the prompt, rubric, and model response completely.

2. Decide on a Quality Rating
   Before making any edits, decide what quality rating (1–5) you would give this task as submitted. You'll use this rating later in Step 6.
   This rating must reflect the task as originally submitted, not after your corrections.

3. Fix the Task
   Correct all issues: prompt errors, rubric errors, missing criteria, wording, weights, atomicity, time-bound issues, ambiguous criteria, inverse pairs, etc.

4. Save Your Changes
   Click Continue on the rubric screen. Do not skip this—otherwise your work is lost.

5. Approve the Task
   Once all issues are fixed, click Approve.

6. Add Error Tags
   Click "Add feedback" next to each block or criterion with errors → Click "Type" on the comment box → Select "Major errors" to access the error tags. Tag ALL errors you identified.
   
   Error Tags:
   - Double Negative: "Does not recommend any laptop that is not lightweight."
   - Duplicate: "Two criteria testing the same 20-liter capacity."
   - Inverse: "Positive ≥18\" height vs. Negative <18\" height."
   - Not measurable: "...with excellent battery life."
   - Not relevant: "...available in stock right now."
   - Self-containment: "...matches the user's concerns mentioned above."
   - Stacked: "...cordless and under 6 lbs and HEPA."
   - Timelessness: "...top-rated TVs of 2024."
   - Unclear Wording: "...a moderately sized suitcase."

   Important: In the comment box, always specify the criterion number and describe the issue. For example: "Criterion 4 is a duplicate" or "Criterion 4 did not relate the color black to the dress".

7. Rate the Task (1–5)
   At the bottom of the task, select your quality rating using the rating you decided in Step 2. This reflects how good the task was before your edits.

8. Leave Feedback (Optional)
   A feedback field will appear. As long as you've added error tags, this is optional—but a quick summary of changes is encouraged.
   To skip, just type anything into the field. Your feedback is shared directly with attempters, so be constructive.

9. Submit Task
   Click "Submit Task" to complete the review. This is required—do not skip this step.

MAJOR ERRORS YOU MUST CATCH
---------------------------
These are critical errors that must be identified and fixed:
- Stacked Criterion: Tests multiple conditions in one item
- Time-Bound Criterion: Violates the timelessness rule
- Redundant Criteria: Two criteria test the same thing
- Missing Criterion: Required +5 or +3 criterion is absent
- Inaccurate Criterion: Contradicts the prompt or includes false info
- Not Self-Contained: Requires external context to grade
- Ambiguous Criterion: Not measurable or open to interpretation
- Restrictive Criterion: Unnecessarily limits valid answers
- Incorrect Weight: Weight doesn't reflect importance
- Incomplete Rubric: Fewer than 15 positive or 3 negative criteria
- Positive/Negative Inverse: Negative is exact opposite of positive

MINOR ERRORS
------------
These don't break the task but should be fixed:
- Criterion doesn't start with action (i.e. "Recommends at least one product")
- Prompt doesn't correctly implement the use case
- Minor grammar or wording mistakes
- Incorrect categorization label

ESCALATING UNSALVAGEABLE TASKS
------------------------------
If you come across a task that is really bad—either the prompt is fundamentally flawed, or the rubric is in such poor shape that it cannot be salvaged—you should escalate it.

⚠️ You cannot edit prompts
Reviewers currently cannot modify prompts. If the prompt itself is flawed (contradictory, unrealistic, too simple, etc.), you must escalate the task—even if the rubric is acceptable.

⚠️ Only escalate rubrics if in a DIRE state
For rubric issues, escalation is reserved for tasks that would take significantly longer than 30 minutes to fix, or where the rubric is so flawed that fixing it would essentially mean creating a new one from scratch.

How to Escalate:
1. Click the three dots (⋮) at the top right of the screen
2. Select "Report task"
3. Choose "Escalation"
4. Add a note explaining why you're escalating (e.g., "Prompt is fundamentally flawed—asks for contradictory requirements" or "Rubric has 12+ major errors and would need complete rewrite")
5. Click "Skip" at the top right to move on to the next task

RATING SCALE (1–5)
------------------
5 - Amazing
Needs 0–1 minutes of edits. Perfect rubric: atomic, measurable, unambiguous. Approve immediately. Flag as exceptional work.

4 - Good
Needs <10 minutes of edits. Strong structure, small mistakes. Approve after light edits.

3 - OK
Needs 10–25 minutes. Clearly good effort but enough issues to require editing. Approve after making fixes.

2 - Bad
Would take 45+ minutes to edit. Clear misunderstanding or major structural problems. Reject (archive), give extensive feedback, flag for SPL follow-up.

1 - Very Bad
Requires complete rewrite. Low effort or spam. Under 10 rubric items. Archive and recommend offboarding.

1. Creating Criteria For Implied Requirements
Error Mode: Improper Criteria

Prompt: "I'm looking for a new house for myself and my wife and two kids. Since there are four of us, the house should have at least four bedrooms and three bathrooms. The square footage should be over 3200, as we like to host parties and get-togethers and want a good amount of space. The lot size should be at least 6000 square feet, giving us enough space to grow a garden and also have outdoor seating and leisure areas. We want to be the first owners of this new home, so it should have been built within the last year or two. The house should be classified as a single-family residential building. Since we have two cars, the garage and driveway should have space for at least two cars. However, we would like more than two cars worth of garage space so that we can have a game room inside it. Since we will most likely be living in a nicer neighborhood, we are happy to pay an HOA fee of up to $320 per month. My wife comes from a hot, humid climate, while the town I grew up in is cold, so we would like the heating and cooling to have controls in each room, since we often disagree on what interior temperature is comfortable. The construction type of the house should be type V, or wood-framed construction, which is standard for houses. We have appliances from our old house that we are happy to bring, so the house need not come with appliances."

Criteria: "Recommends houses that don't come with appliances, since the user is happy to bring their existing appliances to their new residence."

Correction: Remove the criterion.

Rationale: The prompt explicitly states the person doesn't care whether the house comes with appliances or not, so this criterion is inaccurate because there's no evidence to support it.

2. Specifying A Criteria With An Objective - Part 1
Error Mode: Subjective Criteria

Prompt: [Same house prompt as above]

Criteria: "Recommends houses in a safe neighborhood with low crime rates, good night-time lighting, and good accessibility, since the user has two kids."

Correction: "Recommends houses that have a violent crime rate under 200 per 100,000."

Rationale: A "safe" neighborhood is subjective and will be evaluated on how the grader views safety. Instead, we should frame this around an objective metric of crime.

3. Specifying A Criteria With An Objective - Part 2
Error Mode: Subjective Criteria

Prompt: "I live in North Carolina and like to grow my own fruits and vegetable. The earth here is mainly made of clay. I am looking to find a second-hand powered tiller to help break the clay and mix with compost. By the way, my backyard has a slope."

Criteria: "Recommends tiller that can handle tough clay in North Carolina."

Correction: "Recommends a tiller with a minimum engine displacement of 200 cc or greater."

Rationale: This criterion is too subjective – what's considered a tiller that can handle "tough clay"? The statement must be an objective feature of the tiller itself.

If the criterion instead stated: "Recommends a tiller that can handle clay more resistant than 300 PSI" this would still be wrong. Why? Because this is still subjective...what kind of tiller can handle that type of clay? It doesn't say.

4. Using 'Available' Instead of 'Made In' for Sizing
Error Mode: Timelessness

Prompt: "I'm going to a holiday party with some friends in Manhattan and I need to find a dress that's beautiful, memorable, but also sophisticated. I was thinking it should look similar to my favorite movie look of all time: the black, Chantilly lace (with silk underlay) cocktail dress ensemble by Givenchy that Audrey Hepburn wears in the 1966 movie \"How to Steal a Million.\" I have a slender figure like Hepburn, and I'm a size US 4. I'm wondering if you can suggest some black dresses for me with a shift silhouette, with lace or lace detail, which looks similar to the dress in the movie?"

Criteria: "Each recommended dress is available in size US 4 as indicated in the answer text or on the linked product page."

Correction: Replace "available in" with "made in."

Rationale: The word "available" is not timeless—what if the item is out of stock? Stock availability changes constantly. However, "made in" IS timeless because it refers to whether the manufacturer produces that size at all, which is a permanent product attribute.
